# How Summit fits the hackathon

The [official Nerdy AI Hackathon brief](https://hackathon.nerdy.com/) offers a K–5 math direction and an open-idea option. The math direction emphasizes interactive arithmetic, engaging mechanics, progression, and mastery rewards. The open option asks for a genuine learner, a concrete learning problem, and a demonstrable product. **The following is a product assessment, not an official judging score or a prediction of selection.**

Summit's clearest submission framing is **an open-idea learning-through-teaching product, with equal sharing as its first math expedition**. It also answers much of the K–5 math direction. A concise problem statement is: **A correct answer can hide what a child intended and what support they used, leaving parents and tutors without enough evidence for the next useful question.**

## What makes the submission rigorous

The [product guide](PRODUCT-GUIDE.md) defines the learner, hypothesis, proposed success criteria, and iteration decisions. The [architecture](ARCHITECTURE.md) separates frontend interaction, deterministic execution, server persistence, and optional AI. The [verification record](VERIFICATION.md) distinguishes tested software behavior from unmeasured educational outcomes. The [demo](DEMO.md) connects those choices in one visible learner journey.

The next evaluation should measure whether children can create a teaching without an adult operating the controls, explain genuinely new sharing examples with support recorded, and correct a misleading interpretation. Parents and tutors should be able to locate the cited event and choose a relevant next question. Those are proposed checks, not results already achieved. A successful demo is evidence that the product operates; child and adult studies are needed to establish that it helps learning.
